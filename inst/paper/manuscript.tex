\title{Interpretable causal inference for high-stakes settings}
\author{Denis Agniel}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Modern causal inference relies on flexible, black-box nuisance estimation together with sample splitting to obtain valid, efficient inference for treatment effects. In high-stakes settings -- health care payment, treatment triage, sentencing -- that estimation step is itself the object stakeholders need to audit, and a black-box nuisance model, however well it performs, does not offer an audit trail. We propose \emph{doubletree}, an estimator of the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) that replaces the propensity and outcome nuisances with two globally optimal, size-budgeted decision trees, each small enough to display and each fit on every observation, with no sample splitting or cross-fitting for either displayed tree. When the two nuisances are exactly representable by trees within the analyst's leaf budget, doubletree attains the classical semiparametric efficiency bound for the ATT at the parametric $\sqrt n$ rate, and its influence-function variance estimator is the ordinary plug-in, with no jackknife or debiasing correction needed for asymptotically valid inference. This efficiency is not a parametric artifact of fixing the candidate class in advance, since doubletree remains regular and efficient for the full nonparametric model and does not exploit the smaller variance a genuinely parametric restriction would buy. When the leaf budget does not contain the truth, we still maintain valid inference via an approximation-aware confidence interval based on a valid, uninterpretable anchor estimate. The point estimate still relies only on the two auditable trees. Only the confidence interval requires knowledge of the uninterpretable anchor. The underlying tree representation of the nuisances is at least as general and at least as auditable as generalized linear nuisances, and it matches the efficiency of generic double machine learning without requiring cross-fitting.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Causal inference has focused productively on estimators that remain valid under essentially arbitrary nuisance estimation. Augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW) \citep{robins1994,bang2005}, double machine learning \citep{chernozhukov2018}, and targeted maximum likelihood estimation \citep{vanderLaanTargetedLearningCausal2011} are all known to deliver $\sqrt n$-consistent, asymptotically normal estimates of a treatment effect when the propensity score and outcome regression are estimated by any method whose resulting nuisance estimates satisfy a standard product-rate condition and control the own-observation empirical-process term -- in practice by cross-fitting or sample splitting or by a Donsker restriction on the nuisance class \citep{kennedy2016semiparametric}. This robustness to the nuisance-estimation method is what licenses the now-standard practice of pairing these estimators with black-box learners such as random forests or gradient boosting, chosen because they are plausibly assumed to approximate the true nuisance functions under only mild smoothness conditions. In this division of labor, the nuisance model is a means to an end -- a \emph{nuisance} in the technical as well as the colloquial sense -- and is neither expected nor required to be interpreted on its own terms.

That division of labor is unworkable in settings where the stakes of the decision demand that the estimation itself be auditable. If a causal effect estimate is used to set a health care payment adjustment or to inform a triage or sentencing decision, stakeholders may reasonably require that the calculation be traceable step by step, not merely validated by its asymptotic properties. Existing approaches to flexible causal effect estimation do not offer this: they deliver flexible nuisance estimation with asymptotic guarantees, or an interpretable nuisance model, but we are not aware of a construction that delivers both at the efficiency bound without splitting. Furthermore, standard cross-fitting to separate nuisance estimation from evaluation -- even if used with otherwise-interpretable learners -- restricts the auditability of resulting estimators. Cross-fitting encodes randomness in nuisance functions and requires interpreting multiple nuisance fits for each function. That means if 5-fold cross-fitting is used, stakeholders are required to interpret five different propensity score models, for exmple.

To fill this gap without inheriting cross-fitting's own cost to auditability, we propose \emph{doubletree}, an approach to interpretable estimation of the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) that offers flexible, data-adaptive nuisance estimation, full interpretability of every step, and asymptotic guarantees -- together, with no sample splitting for either displayed tree. The name reflects the estimator's structure: one globally optimal decision tree \citep{xuStatisticalOptimalityOptimal2026,mctavishFastSparseDecision2022} for the propensity score and one for the outcome function, each built to a leaf budget that the analyst sets in advance and each small enough, in the intended regime, to fit on an index card. The budget is a single, user-specified interpretability knob: a smaller budget gives a more displayable tree but a coarser approximation, at a price we quantify exactly -- Corollary~\ref{cor:width} shows the reported interval's width plateaus at the product of the two realized nuisance errors when the budget is too small to contain the truth. Under structural sparsity -- when the two nuisances are exactly representable by trees within the leaf budget -- doubletree is $\sqrt n$-consistent and asymptotically normal at the semiparametric efficiency bound for the ATT, with the ordinary plug-in variance estimator and, for asymptotically valid inference, no correction needed for having chosen the tree structure adaptively from the same data used for inference. This efficiency is not a parametric artifact of fixing the candidate class in advance: doubletree remains regular and efficient for the full nonparametric model, and it does not exploit the smaller variance a genuinely parametric restriction on the propensity would buy -- fixing the class restricts the analyst's search, not the statistical model. 

When the true nuisances are not exactly representable within the leaf budget, doubletree maintains appropriate inference by constructing an anchor interval that absorbs the unavoidable approximation bias into its width; the reported point estimate remains the two displayed trees throughout, and only the width, not the estimate, depends on the anchor. The anchor estimate itself is a separate object that, unlike the two displayed trees, need not be interpretable and may use $K$-fold cross-fitting. Even when the leaf budget is too small to contain the truth, doubletree can still target something fixed: when each nuisance has a single best tree-representable approximation within the leaf budget, its point estimate is consistent for the effect that approximation implies. That effect need not equal the true ATT. Inference in either case comes from the anchor interval, which requires neither exact representability nor a unique best approximation -- only a valid $\sqrt n$-consistent anchor.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:setup} sets up notation, identification of the ATT, the tree partition class, and the doubletree algorithm itself. Section~\ref{sec:partition-recovery} isolates the property doubletree's efficiency actually depends on, and Section~\ref{sec:instantiation1} (fixed grid, exact sparsity) delivers it: the central limit theorem for $\thetahat$, its variance estimator, and its regularity and efficiency in the full nonparametric model. Section~\ref{sec:honest-manuscript} addresses what happens, and what can still be claimed, when sparsity fails, and constructs the anchor interval. Section~\ref{sec:finite-discussion} separates what fixing the candidate class does and does not restrict, Section~\ref{sec:single-tree} works out the special case of a single outcome tree with no propensity tree at all, and Section~\ref{sec:lasso-comparison} situates doubletree against penalized linear methods. \section{Setup, identification, and algorithm}
\label{sec:setup}


\subsection{Data, estimand, and notation}

We observe $n$ observations $\bO_1, \ldots, \bO_n$, each an \iid realization of the random variable $\bO = (\bX, A, Y) \sim \Prob$. The observed data $\bO$ consists of a covariate vector $\bX \in \cX \subset \R^p$, a binary treatment $A \in \{0,1\}$, and an outcome $Y \in \R$. Let $Y(a)$ denote the potential outcome under treatment $A = a$. Define the two primary nuisance functions to be the propensity score $e_0(\bx)=\Prob(A=1\mid \bX=\bx)$ and the control ($A=0$) outcome function $\mu_0(\bx)=\E[Y\mid A=0,\bX=\bx]$. Let $\pi=\Prob(A=1)$, $m_1(\bx)=\E[Y\mid A=1,\bX=\bx]$, and $\sigma_a^2(\bx)=\Var(Y\mid A=a,\bX=\bx)$.

Our target parameter is the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:att}
\theta_0 = \E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid A=1].
\end{equation}
The results below are developed for the ATT throughout. Appendix~\ref{app:ate} sketches how the
same identification and bias structure extends to the average treatment effect (ATE).

It is useful to define the following weight when working with the ATT: $w_0(\bx)=e_0(\bx)/\{1-e_0(\bx)\}$, and for a candidate propensity $e(\bx)$, we write
$w(\bx)=e(\bx)/\{1-e(\bx)\}$. We collect the two nuisances into $\eta_0=(e_0,\mu_0)$ and any candidate nuisances into $\eta=(e,\mu)$. We index the two by $j\in\{e,\mu\}$, and we use the notation $\gamma_{0,e}=e_0$ and $\gamma_{0,\mu}=\mu_0$. We write $\Prob_n$ for the empirical measure, $\Prob_n f=n^{-1}\sum_{i\le n}f(\bO_i)$, and
$\pihat=\Prob_n A$. Two norms on functions of $\bx$ are used throughout:
\[
  \twonorm{f}^2=\E\bigl[f(\bX)^2\bigr],
  \qquad
  \|f\|_{2,w}^2=\E\bigl[\{1-e_0(\bX)\}f(\bX)^2\bigr],
\]
the second being the control-weighted norm; the subscript $w$ here records
the control weight $1-e_0$ and is unrelated to the propensity odds $w_0,w$
introduced above. Convergence in distribution is denoted $\rightsquigarrow$
and convergence in probability $\to_p$, and $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ is the standard
normal quantile.


\subsection{Covariate space, tree partitions, and optimal trees}\label{sec:trees}

We do not put a restriction on $\cX$, but we require a
fixed finite collection of permissible cutpoints or category
groupings on each coordinate, such as age brackets, income bands, or clinical
cutoffs. These cutpoints are never updated using the observed data and generate
a finite collection of grid atoms $\cA$, fixed in $n$, with
\[
  M:=\card{\cA}<\infty,
  \qquad
  \Prob(\bX\in a)>0\quad\text{for every }a\in\cA.
\]
This discretization approach is typical in high-stakes applications such as Medicare payment risk-adjustment models, clinical risk scores, and sentencing guidelines \citep{pope2004hcc,wilson1998framingham,breyer1988sentencing}. When continuous covariates are included and not discretized, auditability often militates for a simple linear parametric assumption (which itself is likely violated in practice).

A \emph{tree partition} $\tau$ of $\cX$ is induced by recursive axis-aligned
binary splits (each splitting on one coordinate at a time, so every leaf is a
Cartesian product of per-coordinate intervals or groups -- a box in $\cX$) at
these pre-specified cutpoints only. Its blocks are
\emph{leaves}, each leaf is a union of grid atoms, $\card\tau$ is its leaf
count, and $\ell_\tau(\bx)$ is the leaf containing $\bx$. Further, for leaf $\ell$ define the leaf sample size as $|\ell| = \sum_{i=1}^n \cI\{\bX_i \in \ell\}$, and the control sample size as $|\ell|_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n \cI\{\bX_i \in \ell, A_i = 0\}$. For a strictly positive weight $\nu$ on $\cX$, let $\Pi^\nu_\tau$ denote the $\nu$-weighted projection onto functions constant on the leaves of $\tau$; we write $\Pi_\tau$ for $\Pi^{\Prob}_\tau$, and use the control-weighted projection, $d\nu=\{1-e_0(\bX)\}\,d\Prob$, for the outcome nuisance. Because every leaf is a nonempty union of positive-mass atoms, every candidate leaf automatically has positive probability mass; no separate raw-cell probability floor is required.

We operate under the assumption that a tree is sufficiently interpretable if it obeys a user-specified \emph{leaf budget}, or a maximum number of leaves the tree partition may have. This budget allows the user to determine how complex the tree may become. The saturated grid budget is $\bar L = M$, one leaf per grid atom. In general, stakeholders may prefer a smaller tree, in which case we set the leaf budget at $\bar L \leq M$. Then, define the space of all possible partitions under that budget as
\[
  \cT_{\bar L}=\bigl\{\tau:\tau\text{ a tree partition of }\cX\text{ built
  from the pre-specified cutpoints},\
  \card\tau\le\bar L\bigr\}.
\]
Since the cutpoints and grid atoms are fixed and finite, $\cT_{\bar L}$ is also fixed and finite.

\begin{remark}[The saturated class is representationally maximal]
\label{rem:saturated-general}
At the saturated budget $\Lbar=M$, one leaf per grid atom, a leaf-constant function on $\cT_M$ can take an arbitrary, independently chosen value on every atom: $\cT_M$ therefore represents exactly the functions constant on the atoms of $\cA$ -- call such a function \emph{grid-measurable}. Every model whose fitted value is itself grid-measurable is contained in $\cT_M$ exactly, whatever its functional form: this includes a generalized linear working model fit on grid-derived regressors, since its fitted value is itself constant on the atoms, and by the identical (purely measurability) argument it includes any other functional form -- a random forest, a boosting ensemble, a kernel smoother -- restricted to the same grid-measurable inputs. Against the generalized linear working model specifically, the containment is strict: an interaction across two or more active coordinates that departs from additivity on the working model's link scale is grid-measurable, so a tree with enough leaves represents it exactly, while no choice of coefficients in the working model does. Against a flexible learner grown to purity on the same grid-measurable inputs, the containment is instead generically an \emph{equality} -- such a learner reproduces the same atom-wise fit the saturated tree already delivers, nothing more. The cost of the tree's generality relative to the working model is leaves: for coordinates with $c_1,\ldots,c_k$ grid groups each (inter-cutpoint bins or category groups), contributing additively on the link scale with generic coefficients, the fitted value takes at most $\prod_{i=1}^k c_i$ distinct values across their combinations, exactly that many for generic coefficients ($2^k$ when every active coordinate is binary), and because leaves are axis-aligned unions of atoms rather than arbitrary level sets, representing it exactly generally requires a separate leaf for each one. Ties \emph{across} coordinates do not reduce the leaf count, since the level sets they induce are typically not themselves axis-aligned unions of grid atoms; ties \emph{within} a coordinate, between adjacent groups, can. This is a genuine cost only once $\Lbar$ falls below that product; it does not arise at the saturated budget, and \S\ref{sec:discussion} and Corollary~\ref{cor:width} return to what a smaller, legible budget gives up.
\end{remark}

Finally, we define for any function $\gamma$ a \emph{sufficient class}, or a class of trees that are sufficient to describe the function comprehensively:
 \begin{definition}[Sufficient class]\label{def:sufficient}
For $j\in\{e,\mu\}$ and any positive weight vector $\nu$,
\[
  \cS_j=\bigl\{\tau\in\cT_{\Lbar}:
  \Pi^\nu_\tau\gamma_{0,j}=\gamma_{0,j}\ \ \Prob\text{-a.s.}\bigr\},
\]
equivalently the set of $\tau\in\cT_{\Lbar}$ on whose leaves $\gamma_{0,j}$ is
$\Prob$-a.s.\ constant.
\end{definition}

\begin{assumption}[Structural sparsity]\label{ass:sparsity}
$\cS_e\ne\emptyset$ and $\cS_\mu\ne\emptyset$: some tree in $\cT_{\Lbar}$
represents the propensity exactly, and some tree in $\cT_{\Lbar}$ represents
the control-outcome regression exactly, each in the sense of
Definition~\ref{def:sufficient}.
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity} is a sparsity condition, not a
correct-specification condition: it does not require $e_0$ or $\mu_0$ to
take any particular functional form, only that each be exactly constant on
the leaves of some tree built from the pre-specified grid, at or below the
leaf budget $\Lbar$. \S\ref{sec:finite-discussion} returns to what fixing
the candidate class in advance does and does not restrict.

\begin{remark}[Legibility does not require sparsity]
\label{rem:legibility-not-complete}
A legible leaf budget $\Lbar$ bounds every fitted tree at $\Lbar$ leaves --
small enough to display and for stakeholders to audit --
regardless of whether Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity} holds, since
$\cT_{\Lbar}$ is fixed and finite by construction whatever $\cS_e$ and
$\cS_\mu$ turn out to be. Thus, even if Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity} does not hold, we still provide two auditable $\Lbar$-sized trees. 
When the assumption fails, the displayed trees remain fully auditable
objects, but what they display is a tree-representable approximation to
$e_0$ and $\mu_0$, not $e_0$ and $\mu_0$ themselves. In these cases, we accept the approximation bias as the price of legibility, and we give an alternative approach for inference that acknowledges this bias.\end{remark}

 \subsection{Identification and influence function}
 
In this section, we briefly review how the causal effect of interest is identified. 

\begin{assumption}[Causal identification]\label{ass:causal}
Consistency, $Y=AY(1)+(1-A)Y(0)$; ignorability,
$\{Y(0),Y(1)\}\perp A\mid \bX$; $\pi>0$; and there exists $c\in(0,1)$ with
\[
  1-e_0(\bx)\ \ge\ c \qquad\text{for every }\bx\in\cX .
\]
\end{assumption}

Versions of this set of assumptions are standard in the causal inference literature, though we require a \emph{uniform} lower bound $1-e_0(\bx)\ge c$ for every $\bx\in\cX$, rather than the weaker pointwise condition $e_0(\bx)<1$ that suffices in some other treatments. On a finite covariate space the two are essentially interchangeable, since a maximum over finitely many finite values is automatically finite. On a general (e.g.\ continuous) $\cX$ they are not: expanding $V=\E[\psi_0^2]$ from the efficient influence function given below (Eq.~\eqref{eq:score}) by conditioning on $A$ shows the resulting variance contains a term $\E[e_0(\bX)^2\sigma_0^2(\bX)/\{1-e_0(\bX)\}]$, which can diverge under only pointwise positivity if $P_{\bX}$ places mass where $1-e_0(\bx)\to0$ without a compensating decay in the control-outcome variance $\sigma_0^2(\bx)$. The uniform bound $c$ is what keeps this term finite, not merely a convenience. Under Assumption \ref{ass:causal}, we may identify the ATT by the following observed data quantity:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:att-id}
	\theta_0 = \E\left\{Y - \E\left(Y | \bX, A = 0\right) | A = 1\right\}.
\end{align}
The efficient influence function for this functional in the nonparametric model is
\citep{hahn1998}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:score}
  \psi(\bO;\theta,\eta)
  =\frac1\pi\Bigl[A\bigl\{Y-\mu(\bX)-\theta\bigr\}
   -(1-A)\,w(\bX)\bigl\{Y-\mu(\bX)\bigr\}\Bigr],
  \qquad w=\frac e{1-e},
\end{equation}
and we write $\psi_0=\psi(\cdot;\theta_0,\eta_0)$. We will use this quantity to build a new estimator for the ATT based on interpretable trees in the next section.

\subsection{The doubletree algorithm}
To build an interpretable causal estimate, we use globally optimal interpretable trees to estimate both required nuisance functions $e_0, \mu_0$, and -- since this approach relies on two trees -- we call our estimation approach the \emph{doubletree} algorithm.

Doubletree selects the tree from $\cT_{\bar L}$ that minimizes the penalized empirical risk over the space of feasible trees. We take the space of feasible trees to be a subset of $\cT_{\bar L}$ for regularity reasons. Specifically, define the feasible sets as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:feasible}
  \cT^{(\mu)}_n=\bigl\{\tau\in\cT_{\bar L}: \min_{\ell \in \tau} |\ell|_0 \geq m_n\bigr\}, \qquad 
    \cT^{(e)}_n=\bigl\{\tau\in\cT_{\bar L}: \min_{\ell \in \tau} |\ell| \geq m_n\bigr\},
\end{equation}
and for a penalty $\lambda_n>0$ set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:select}
  \that_j\in\argmin_{\tau\in\cT^{(j)}_n}
  \bigl\{R^{(j)}_n(\tau)+\lambda_n\card\tau\bigr\},
  \qquad j\in\{e,\mu\},
\end{equation}
where $R^{(j)}_n(\tau)$ is the empirical risk of tree $\tau$ for nuisance $j$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:risk-emp}
R^{(e)}_n(\tau)=\Prob_n\bigl[\{A-\hat e_\tau(\bX)\}^2\bigr],
\qquad
R^{(\mu)}_n(\tau)=\frac{1}{n_0}\sum_{i:A_i=0}\bigl\{Y_i-\hat\mu_\tau(\bX_i)\bigr\}^2,
\end{equation}
squared error for both the propensity tree and the outcome tree, with $n_0=\sum_{i\le n}(1-A_i)$ and leaf-wise empirical plug-ins
\begin{equation}\label{eq:refit}
\hat e_\tau(\bx)=\frac{1}{|\ell_\tau(\bx)|}\sum_{i:\bX_i\in\ell_\tau(\bx)}A_i,
\qquad
\hat\mu_\tau(\bx)=\frac{1}{|\ell_\tau(\bx)|_0}\sum_{i:\bX_i\in\ell_\tau(\bx),\,A_i=0}Y_i ,
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the empirical proportion treated and the empirical control-outcome mean within the leaf containing $\bx$. Given leaf-refit nuisances $(\hat e_{\hat\tau_e},\hat\mu_{\hat\tau_\mu})$ for the selected partitions $\hat\tau_e,\hat\tau_\mu$, the doubletree estimate $\thetahat=\thetahat(\hat\tau_e,\hat\tau_\mu)$ is the solution of the empirical estimating equation $\Prob_n[\psi(\bO;\theta,\hat e_{\hat\tau_e},\hat\mu_{\hat\tau_\mu})]=0$ for $\psi$ as in \eqref{eq:score}, i.e.\
\[
  \thetahat
  =
  \frac{1}{n\hat\pi}\sum_{i=1}^n
  \Bigl[A_i\{Y_i-\hat\mu_{\hat\tau_\mu}(\bX_i)\}
    -\{1-A_i\}\hat w_{\hat\tau_e}(\bX_i)\{Y_i-\hat\mu_{\hat\tau_\mu}(\bX_i)\}\Bigr],
\]
with $\hat\pi=\Prob_n A$ and $\hat w_{\hat\tau_e}=\hat e_{\hat\tau_e}/(1-\hat e_{\hat\tau_e})$; the root is unique whenever $\hat\pi>0$, since $\psi$ is affine and strictly monotone in $\theta$. The quantity $R^{(j)}(\tau)$ is the population counterpart of $R^{(j)}_n(\tau)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:risk-pop}
R^{(e)}(\tau)=\E\bigl[\{A-\Pi_\tau e_0(\bX)\}^2\bigr],
\qquad
R^{(\mu)}(\tau)=\E\bigl[\{Y-\Pi^\nu_\tau\mu_0(\bX)\}^2\mid A=0\bigr],
\end{equation}
obtained from \eqref{eq:risk-emp} by replacing the empirical measure with the population measure and the empirical leaf plug-ins $\hat e_\tau,\hat\mu_\tau$ with the projections $\Pi_\tau e_0$ and $\Pi^\nu_\tau\mu_0$ introduced above. These projections are exactly the squared-error minimizers.

\begin{remark}
Note that we are using a flexible tree learner to estimate both nuisance functions, but we do not require sample-splitting/cross-fitting as most approaches that use data-adaptive nuisance learners do \citep{chernozhukov2018}. This means that doubletree will make more efficient use of potentially limited data. It also means that doubletree is fully explained by exactly two trees. Under $K$-fold cross-fitting, users would need to interpret 2$K$ trees, as opposed to doubletree's two.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why two trees: interpretability and identification are separate questions]
\label{rem:two-trees-why}
The account above motivates two trees on interpretability grounds -- doubletree is exactly displayable as two objects -- but interpretability alone does not explain why \emph{efficiency} or \emph{validity} requires both nuisances to be estimated by a tree. Two points are worth separating precisely. First, the propensity \emph{tree} specifically is an interpretability choice, not by itself a validity requirement: the exact bias decomposition stated below in \S\ref{sec:honest-manuscript} (Proposition~\ref{prop:bilinear}, Lemma~\ref{lem:biasbound}) shows that only the \emph{product} of the two realized nuisance errors, $D_wD_\mu$ in that lemma's notation, needs to vanish fast enough, and that product-rate condition is indifferent to whether $\hat w$ is itself piecewise constant on a tree. This is necessary but not sufficient on its own: because doubletree performs no sample splitting or cross-fitting, asymptotic linearity of $\thetahat$ also requires the own-observation empirical-process term $(\Prob_n-\Prob)\{\psi(\cdot;\theta_0,\hat\eta)-\psi_0\}$ to be $o_p(n^{-1/2})$, and it is exactly to control \emph{that} term without splitting that the fixed, finite candidate class $\cT_{\Lbar}$ and exact structural sparsity on it are needed (\S\ref{sec:partition-recovery}--\S\ref{sec:instantiation1}). An arbitrary non-tree $\hat w$ fit on the same $n$ observations and satisfying only the product-rate condition is not in general covered by this argument. Second, the propensity \emph{nuisance} itself is genuinely informative for the ATT, unlike for the ATE: \citet{hahn1998} shows that knowledge of $e_0$ does not lower the semiparametric efficiency bound for the average treatment effect, but it does lower it for the average treatment effect on the treated -- so dropping the propensity is not a free simplification for the estimand considered here. Corollary~\ref{cor:single-tree} and its consequences below make both points precise in the concrete special case of a single outcome tree fit with no propensity tree at all.
\end{remark}

Additionally, we assume that the fitted propensities agree with the positivity assumption in \ref{ass:causal}:
\begin{assumption}[Clipping]\label{ass:construct}
Fitted propensities are clipped so that $1-\hat e(\bx)\ge c$ for every $\bx$, with $c$ the constant of Assumption~\ref{ass:causal}.
\end{assumption}

\section{Doubletree bias and inference}

\subsection{Partition recovery and the central limit theorem}
\label{sec:partition-recovery}

Doubletree's efficiency rests entirely on whether the two fitted tree
partitions recover the population structure of the two nuisances -- not on
any property of the grid used to search for them. We isolate exactly this
requirement and deliver it in its most direct form for the analyst's own
pre-specified grid: exact structural sparsity, under which selection lands,
with probability tending to one, in the finite class of partitions that are
already exactly right, and the resulting central limit theorem follows by
uniform asymptotic linearity over that class
(\S\ref{sec:instantiation1}). This is a special case of a more general
partition-recovery condition, Condition~(P), under which the fitted
partitions need only converge to \emph{some} fixed reference partition --
reassigning a bounded number of observations along the way -- rather than
landing exactly on one from the start. We do not develop Condition~(P) in
this paper: the grid the analyst
searches is finite by construction (\S\ref{sec:trees}), so the direct
sparsity argument applies without passing through Condition~(P) at all.

\subsection{Instantiation 1: fixed grid, exact sparsity}
\label{sec:instantiation1}

We are in this setting when Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity} holds: both
nuisances are exactly representable by trees that observe the leaf budget
$\Lbar$ on the analyst's own pre-specified grid. This is the expository
case: the grid is finite by construction (\S\ref{sec:trees}), so once
selection lands in the sufficient class, partition recovery is exact. The resulting central
limit theorem is proved by uniform asymptotic linearity over the whole
sufficient class, not by verifying Condition~(P) or comparing the selected
partition with a single reference partition -- see the discussion following
Proposition~\ref{lem:uniform} below.

First, note that the doubletree nuisance functions will land in the
sufficient class with probability tending to 1:

\begin{proposition}[Selection lands in the sufficient class]
\label{lem:selection}
Suppose the observations are \iid; Assumptions~\ref{ass:causal} and
\ref{ass:construct} hold; the conditional second moments of $Y$ are uniformly
bounded; the pre-specified cutpoints and their induced positive-mass grid
atoms are fixed and finite in $n$, and optimization considers only partitions
built from those cutpoints; $\Lbar$ is fixed;
$\Lbar m_n\lesssim n$; and $\tauhat_j$ is an exact global minimizer
of~\eqref{eq:select}, with $\lambda_n\to0$. For each $j\in\{e,\mu\}$, if
$\cS_j\ne\emptyset$, then
\[
  \Prob\bigl(\tauhat_j\in\cS_j\bigr)\longrightarrow1 ;
\]
in particular, under Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity} this holds for both $j$
simultaneously.
\end{proposition}

This proposition ensures that $\tauhat_j$ is consistent for the true sufficient partition for both nuisances $j = e, \mu$. If we furthermore have $Y$ bounded, we can establish the rate of this selection consistency:

\begin{proposition}[Exponential selection bound]
\label{prop:selection-rate}
Under the conditions of Proposition~\ref{lem:selection}, suppose additionally
that $Y$ is bounded. Define
\[
  \Delta_j
  =
  \min_{\tau\in\cT_{\Lbar}\setminus\cS_j}
  \left\{
    R^{(j)}(\tau)
    -
    \min_{\tau'\in\cT_{\Lbar}}R^{(j)}(\tau')
  \right\}.
\]
Then $\Delta_j>0$, and for some constant $c_1>0$, depending only on the
outcome bound, $c$, and the positive leaf masses of the relevant sufficient
partitions,
\[
  \Prob\bigl(\tauhat_j\notin\cS_j\bigr)
  \le
  \card{\cT_{\Lbar}}\exp\{-c_1n\Delta_j^2\},
  \qquad j\in\{e,\mu\}.
\]
\end{proposition}

The consistency of the nuisances implies the consistency of the doubletree estimator:

\begin{corollary}[Consistency]
\label{cor:consistency}
Under the conditions of Proposition~\ref{lem:selection},
\[
  \thetahat-\theta_0=o_p(1).
\]
\end{corollary}

We now establish the central limit theorem for $\thetahat$ directly, by an
argument that does not go through Condition~(P) at all: because the
grid-exact sufficient class $\cS_j$ need not be a singleton, we establish
uniform asymptotic linearity over the \emph{whole} class, rather than
anchoring at one fixed reference partition, which a more general argument
along the lines of Condition~(P) would instead require.

\begin{proposition}[Uniform asymptotic linearity on the sufficient class]
\label{lem:uniform}
For $(\tau_e,\tau_\mu)\in\cT_{\Lbar}^2$, let
$\thetahat(\tau_e,\tau_\mu)$ denote the estimator formed from the corresponding
full-sample leaf refits. Under the conditions of Proposition~\ref{lem:selection},
\[
  \max_{(\tau_e,\tau_\mu)\in\cS_e\times\cS_\mu}
  \left|
    \sqrt n\{\thetahat(\tau_e,\tau_\mu)-\theta_0\}
    -
    \sqrt n\,\Prob_n\psi_0
  \right|
  =o_p(1).
\]
Exact global optimization and the rate of $\lambda_n$ are not required for
this uniform statement.
\end{proposition}

This proposition establishes an asymptotic equivalence between the normalized empirical mean of the influence function and the normalized error for \emph{every} estimator based on nuisances drawn from the sufficient classes. This result is a statement not yet about the particular doubletree estimator but about any estimator using sufficient nuisances.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-grid central limit theorem by uniform linearity]
\label{prop:P-instantiation1}
Suppose the conditions of Proposition~\ref{lem:uniform} hold -- in
particular Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity} -- and $V:=\E[\psi_0^2]>0$. Then
\[
  \sqrt n(\thetahat-\theta_0)\rightsquigarrow N(0,V).
\]
This conclusion follows independently of Condition~(P): no (P1) or (P2)
verification is asserted, and in particular the argument needs no unique
reference partition, only that selection lands in the (possibly
non-singleton) sufficient class.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{lem:selection}, $\tauhat_j\in\cS_j$ for both $j=e,\mu$
with probability tending to one. On that event, Proposition~\ref{lem:uniform}
gives
\[
  \sqrt n(\thetahat-\theta_0)
  =
  \sqrt n\,\Prob_n\psi_0+o_p(1).
\]
The classical i.i.d.\ central limit theorem and Slutsky's theorem give the
stated conclusion, with
$V>0$ by hypothesis.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Variance estimation and confidence intervals]
\label{cor:variance}
Under the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:P-instantiation1}, let
\[
  \Vhat
  =
  \frac{1}{n\pihat^2}
  \sum_{i=1}^n
  \left[
    A_i\{Y_i-\hat\mu(\bX_i)-\thetahat\}
    -
    (1-A_i)\hat w(\bX_i)\{Y_i-\hat\mu(\bX_i)\}
  \right]^2,
  \qquad
  \hat w=\frac{\hat e}{1-\hat e}.
\]
Then $\Vhat\to_p V$, and
\[
  \thetahat
  \pm
  z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\Vhat/n}
\]
has pointwise asymptotic coverage $1-\alpha$ for $\theta_0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Write $\Vhat(\tau_e,\tau_\mu)$ for the statistic above formed from the
leaf refits $(\hat e_{\tau_e},\hat\mu_{\tau_\mu})$ at a fixed pair
$(\tau_e,\tau_\mu)$, so that $\Vhat=\Vhat(\hat\tau_e,\hat\tau_\mu)$. For every
fixed $(\tau_e,\tau_\mu)\in\cS_e\times\cS_\mu$, these leaf refits converge to
$(e_0,\mu_0)$ on the finitely many leaves of $(\tau_e,\tau_\mu)$, and
Assumption~\ref{ass:construct} keeps $1-\hat e$ bounded away from zero
throughout, so $\hat w=\hat e/(1-\hat e)$ is uniformly bounded by $(1-c)/c$.
Because each summand of $\Vhat(\tau_e,\tau_\mu)$ is a function of these
finitely many, uniformly bounded leaf-wise plug-ins, the law of large
numbers applies leaf by leaf and, together with the uniformly bounded
conditional second moment of $Y$, gives
$\Vhat(\tau_e,\tau_\mu)\to_p\E[\psi_0^2]=V$; since $\cS_e\times\cS_\mu$ is
fixed and finite, this convergence is uniform over it. By
Proposition~\ref{lem:selection}, the selected partitions land in
$\cS_e\times\cS_\mu$ with probability tending to one, so $\Vhat\to_pV$.
Combining this with Proposition~\ref{prop:P-instantiation1} and Slutsky's
theorem gives the stated coverage.
\end{proof}
These results justify using the typical variance and confidence interval
approaches used throughout EIF-based causal inference. Because both tree
structures and their leaf values are fitted on all $n$ observations,
$\Vhat$'s summands are in-sample residuals, so $\Vhat$ is downward biased in
finite samples by a relative factor of order $(2\bar L+1)/n$; a
degrees-of-freedom divisor $n-(\card{\that_e}+\card{\that_\mu}+1)$ is a
reasonable finite-sample convention, though the accompanying software
reports the plain $n$-normalised plug-in by default, to keep doubletree
directly comparable to the other estimators it ships alongside, and leaves
the rescaling to the user.

This central limit theorem also settles a question of efficiency, not merely consistency: is the fixed, finite candidate class itself a hidden parametric restriction that buys a smaller variance than the fully nonparametric model allows?

\begin{theorem}[Regularity and efficiency]
\label{thm:regular}
Under the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:P-instantiation1}, $\thetahat$
is regular and locally asymptotically efficient for $\theta_0$ in the full
nonparametric model consistent with Assumption~\ref{ass:causal}'s one-sided
positivity bound $1-e_0\ge c$: no estimator that is regular along every
parametric submodel through the true law can improve on the asymptotic
variance $V$. No additional restriction on $e_0$ -- in particular, no lower
bound on $e_0$ itself -- is required: differentiating the ATT's
observed-data representation \eqref{eq:att-id}
directly needs only the control regression $\mu_0$, which
Assumption~\ref{ass:causal} already keeps well defined everywhere, and never
$m_1(\bx)$, which would be undefined wherever $e_0(\bx)=0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proposition}[The submodel bound is not exploited]
\label{prop:sparse-bound}
Under the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:P-instantiation1}, fix
$\tau\in\cS_e$ and let $\cP_\tau$ denote the much smaller submodel in which
$e_0$ is assumed to be exactly constant on the leaves of $\tau$. The
semiparametric efficiency bound for $\theta_0$ in $\cP_\tau$ is $V_\tau\le
V$, strictly smaller than $V$ whenever the treated--control contrast varies
within a leaf of $\tau$ on which $e_0>0$: exact knowledge of the tree
structure would genuinely lower the achievable variance. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:regular}, the doubletree estimator does not exploit this --
it remains regular and attains $V$, the bound for the full model, not
$V_\tau$.
\end{proposition}

Doubletree therefore attains the full nonparametric efficiency bound, not the smaller bound that a genuinely parametric restriction on $e_0$ and $\mu_0$ would buy. \S\ref{sec:finite-discussion} returns to what this does, and does not, say about fixing the candidate class in advance.
\subsection{When partition recovery fails: the anchor interval}
\label{sec:honest-manuscript}
Structural sparsity holding delivers efficiency (\S\ref{sec:partition-recovery}--\S\ref{sec:instantiation1}); this section addresses what happens when it may not hold. We are in the non-sparse setting when at least one sufficient class is empty: $\cS_e = \emptyset$ or $\cS_\mu = \emptyset$. Here, at least one nuisance is not exactly representable by a tree that observes the leaf budget $\Lbar$, so we should expect some bias in $\thetahat$ relative to $\theta_0$. The interval constructed below is, in spirit, an honest, approximation-aware confidence interval in the tradition of \citet{armstrong2018}: rather than assuming the bias away or estimating it directly, it is bounded and absorbed into the interval's width.

Before turning to the fully general case, it is worth isolating a setting where the non-sparse case still has a well-defined target. When the population risk over $\cT_{\Lbar}$ has a unique minimizer for each nuisance, doubletree's own selection procedure finds it, and the point estimate is consistent for the effect that minimizer implies -- even though that effect need not equal $\theta_0$.

\begin{assumption}[Pseudo-true margin]\label{ass:pseudo}
For each $j\in\{e,\mu\}$, there is a unique $\tau_j^\dagger\in\cT_{\Lbar}$ minimizing $R^{(j)}$ over $\cT_{\Lbar}$, and
\[
  \Delta_j^\dagger := \min_{\tau\in\cT_{\Lbar}\setminus\{\tau_j^\dagger\}} \bigl\{R^{(j)}(\tau) - R^{(j)}(\tau_j^\dagger)\bigr\} > 0.
\]
Write $\eta_*=(e_*,\mu_*)$, with $e_*=e_{\tau_e^\dagger}$ and $\mu_*=\Pi^\nu_{\tau_\mu^\dagger}\mu_0$ in the notation of \S\ref{sec:instantiation1} -- the two components indexed by their own, generally distinct, partitions -- and let $\theta_*$ solve $\E[\psi(\bO;\theta,\eta_*)]=0$; this solution is unique because $\psi$ is affine and strictly monotone in $\theta$ (\S\ref{sec:trees}--\S\ref{sec:setup}).
\end{assumption}

Because $\cT_{\Lbar}$ is finite, the margin $\Delta_j^\dagger$ is automatically positive once the minimizer $\tau_j^\dagger$ is unique -- a minimum over finitely many strictly positive numbers cannot be zero -- so uniqueness is the substantive content of Assumption~\ref{ass:pseudo}, not a separate condition alongside it. Assumptions~\ref{ass:sparsity} and~\ref{ass:pseudo} are non-nested rather than ordered: Assumption~\ref{ass:pseudo} drops exactness, requiring only that the best tree-representable approximation to each nuisance be unique, but it is not implied by Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity}, since every $\tau\in\cS_j$ attains the same population risk $R^{(j)}(\tau)$ by Definition~\ref{def:sufficient} -- so a non-singleton sufficient class is exactly an exact tie, and structural sparsity generically produces one whenever $\cS_j$ is not a singleton. Assumption~\ref{ass:pseudo} is therefore the relevant hypothesis when sparsity fails and the best approximation is nonetheless unique -- the case this subsection treats -- not a weakening of Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity}. It fails only through an exact tie: if two or more partitions of $\cT_{\Lbar}$ attain the same best population risk, $\tau_j^\dagger$ is undefined and the proposition below does not apply. A near-tie leaves Assumption~\ref{ass:pseudo} intact -- the conclusion below still holds -- and only slows how quickly it kicks in, through the exponent of Proposition~\ref{prop:selection-rate}.

\begin{proposition}[Consistency at the pseudo-true partitions]
\label{prop:pseudo-consistency}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:pseudo} and the conditions of Proposition~\ref{lem:selection} other than Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity},
\[
  \Prob(\tauhat_j=\tau_j^\dagger)\longrightarrow 1, \qquad j\in\{e,\mu\},
\]
consequently $\hat\eta\to_p\eta_*$, and $\thetahat-\theta_*=o_p(1)$. This is the same condition Proposition~\ref{prop:spectest} below invokes to power its consistency direction; there $\hat\eta\to_p\eta_*$ is a hypothesis, whereas here it is derived.
\end{proposition}

The mechanism is exactly Proposition~\ref{lem:selection}'s: that argument uses only that a finite candidate class has a feasible population risk minimizer separated from every competitor by a positive margin, and compares the penalized empirical objective against it -- nothing about the minimizer being exactly right for $\gamma_{0,j}$, only that it is the best available and uniquely so. Assumption~\ref{ass:pseudo} supplies $\tau_j^\dagger$ and $\Delta_j^\dagger$ in exactly the roles $\cS_j$ and $\Delta_j$ play in the sparse case, so the same comparison gives selection landing on $\tau_j^\dagger$ with probability tending to one. Once selection lands there, the fitted nuisances are leaf refits on a fixed partition and converge to their population leaf values $e_*,\mu_*$ by the same law of large numbers used for the tied estimator's fixed-partition expansion (\S\ref{sec:instantiation1}); $1-e_*\ge c$ by Assumption~\ref{ass:causal}, so the limit is well behaved. Substituting these limits into the estimating equation and using that $\psi$ is affine in $\theta$ gives $\thetahat\to_p\theta_*$.

This is consistency, not a central limit theorem: establishing $\sqrt n$-normality of $\thetahat-\theta_*$, with a variance one could actually compute, would require an off-sparsity linearity result -- extending the argument underlying the sparse-case central limit theorem (Proposition~\ref{lem:uniform}), which is proved only over the sufficient class, to the estimated partition when it lands outside it. We do not supply that extension here, and we do not characterize how difficult it is to obtain; it is left as an open question. Nor is Assumption~\ref{ass:pseudo} itself contained the way sparsity's failure is: when structural sparsity fails, $\thetahat$ remains well-defined and Theorem~\ref{thm:anchor} below stays valid regardless. When Assumption~\ref{ass:pseudo} fails -- through an exact tie -- the target $\theta_*$ itself may not exist, and the selected partitions can oscillate with no fixed limit at all. The anchor interval constructed next does not rely on Assumption~\ref{ass:pseudo}, or on $\thetahat$ converging to anything fixed: it is valid whether or not a pseudo-true partition exists.

We begin with an exact decomposition of this bias:

\begin{proposition}[Exact bilinear remainder]
\label{prop:bilinear}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:causal}, for any
$\eta=(e,\mu)$ satisfying $1-e(\bx)\ge c$ for every $\bx\in\cX$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bilinear}
  \E\bigl[\psi(\bO;\theta_0,\eta)\bigr]
  =
  \frac{1}{\pi}
  \E\left[
    \{1-e_0(\bX)\}
    \{w_0(\bX)-w(\bX)\}
    \{\mu_0(\bX)-\mu(\bX)\}
  \right].
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

This decomposition shows that the bias of $\thetahat$ is exactly a bilinear remainder in the errors of the two fitted nuisances -- the same mechanism that gives doubly robust estimators their name. Bounding each nuisance's error in the appropriate norm gives an explicit bound on the size of that bias:

\begin{lemma}[Bias bound in the control-weighted norm]
\label{lem:biasbound}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:causal} and~\ref{ass:construct}, define the
realized nuisance errors
\[
  D_w=\|\hat w-w_0\|_{2,w},
  \qquad
  D_\mu=\|\hat\mu-\mu_0\|_{2,w}.
\]
Then the population estimating-equation bias evaluated at the fitted
nuisances satisfies
\[
  \left|
    \left.
    \E\bigl[\psi(\bO;\theta_0,\eta)\bigr]
    \right|_{\eta=\hat\eta}
  \right|
  \le
  \frac{1}{\pi}D_wD_\mu .
\]
\end{lemma}

This bound shows that $|\thetahat - \theta_0|$ is controlled, up to constants, by the product of the two realized nuisance errors $D_w D_\mu$ -- not directly by the population approximation errors $\delta_e, \delta_\mu$, which measure only the best achievable bias at budget $\Lbar$ and need not equal what the fitted trees actually attain. When $D_w D_\mu$ is $o(n^{-1/2})$, $\thetahat$ remains asymptotically efficient even outside the sparse setting; but in general we cannot guarantee this rate, so we cannot guarantee that the usual normal-theory confidence interval is valid.

We therefore need an inferential strategy that is valid regardless of $D_w D_\mu$. The key idea is to rely on a valid, non-interpretable \emph{anchor} estimate, $\thetatilde$: a second estimator that is $\sqrt n$-consistent and asymptotically normal without requiring sparsity. The anchor might be what you would choose outside of high-stakes settings requiring interpretability. In this case, it will help us obtain inference for our interpretable estimate. The anchor need not use optimal trees or cross-fitting, it need only be $\sqrt n$-consistent, but one natural choice is a cross-fit estimator using optimal trees, which under standard nuisance-rate and empirical-process control conditions satisfies these properties by the same cross-fitting argument used throughout the double-machine-learning literature \citep{chernozhukov2018}.

\begin{assumption}[Anchor nuisance rate]\label{ass:rate}
When the anchor is constructed from $K$ fold-wise nuisance fits, with $K$
fixed, let $\widetilde e^{(-k)}$ and $\widetilde\mu^{(-k)}$ denote the
propensity and outcome fits trained without fold $k$. These fits, which need
not use optimal trees, satisfy
\[
  \max_{1\le k\le K}
  \twonorm{\widetilde e^{(-k)}-e_0}\,
  \bigl\|\widetilde\mu^{(-k)}-\mu_0\bigr\|_{2,w}
  =
  o_p(n^{-1/2}).
\]
An anchor constructed by another method may instead be used if it directly
satisfies the $\sqrt n$-consistency and asymptotic normality conditions stated
in Theorem~\ref{thm:anchor}.
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{ass:rate} concerns the anchor rather than the displayed
doubletree nuisances. It is not made automatic merely by choosing the
saturated grid budget $\Lbar=M$: when $\cX$ is general, saturation separates
all grid atoms but cannot force the nuisance functions to be constant within
those atoms. With a valid anchor $\thetatilde$ in hand, we can construct an
interval centered at the doubletree estimate itself, widened just enough to
remain valid regardless of the size of the bias:

\begin{theorem}[Anchor interval]
\label{thm:anchor}
Suppose
\[
  \sqrt n(\thetatilde-\theta_0)
  \rightsquigarrow N(0,\widetilde V),
  \qquad
  \widetilde V>0,
  \qquad
  \widetilde\sigma^2\to_p\widetilde V,
\]
and define $\widehat\delta=\thetahat-\thetatilde$. Then
\[
  C_n
  =
  \left[
    \thetahat
    \pm
    \left\{
      \abs{\widehat\delta}
      +
      z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\widetilde\sigma}{\sqrt n}
    \right\}
  \right]
\]
satisfies
\[
  \liminf_{n\to\infty}\Prob(\theta_0\in C_n)\ge1-\alpha
\]
on $\{\pihat>0\}$, whose probability tends to one. No rate or smoothness
condition on the doubletree nuisances is required.
\end{theorem}

This interval retains $\thetahat$, and hence the two displayed trees, as the point estimate; only the width, not the point estimate, absorbs the unknown bias. The following corollary characterizes that width explicitly:

\begin{corollary}[Width]
\label{cor:width}
Suppose the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:anchor} hold, the doubletree
nuisances satisfy the conditions of Proposition~\ref{lem:selection},
and the anchor is $\sqrt n$-efficient. With $D_w, D_\mu$ as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:biasbound}, we have
\[
  \abs{\widehat\delta}
  =
  O_p(D_wD_\mu+n^{-1/2}),
  \qquad
  \operatorname{width}(C_n)
  =
  O_p\!\left(\max\{n^{-1/2},D_wD_\mu\}\right).
\]
In particular, the interval has width $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ whenever
$D_wD_\mu=O_p(n^{-1/2})$.
\end{corollary}

This corollary shows that the interval contracts at the usual parametric rate whenever $D_w D_\mu = O(n^{-1/2})$, and otherwise plateaus at a width set by the product of the realized nuisance errors -- an explicit price for insisting on a small, displayable tree when the truth does not fall in the interpretable class.

Since $\delta_e, \delta_\mu$ are unknown in practice, it is useful to have a diagnostic for whether the sparse setting plausibly holds at all:

\begin{proposition}[Fidelity diagnostic]
\label{prop:spectest}
Under the conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:P-instantiation1}, together with the anchor's
fold-wise nuisance rate condition (Assumption~\ref{ass:rate}),
\[
  \sqrt n\,\widehat\delta=o_p(1).
\]
Consequently, for any
$\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}\asymp n^{-1/2}$, the test that rejects sparsity when
\[
  \abs{\widehat\delta}
  >
  z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}
\]
has size tending to zero. If, under an alternative, Assumption~\ref{ass:pseudo} holds -- so that, by
Proposition~\ref{prop:pseudo-consistency}, $\hat\eta\to_p\eta_*$ -- the test is
consistent whenever
\[
  b:=\E\bigl[\psi(\bO;\theta_0,\eta_*)\bigr]\ne0 .
\]
\end{proposition}

Together, these results mean that a user need never choose, in advance, between reporting an interpretable estimator and reporting a valid one: the same two trees are displayed and used as the point estimate whether or not sparsity holds, and the diagnostic together with the anchor interval's width tell the user how much confidence to place in efficiency versus mere validity.

\subsection{What the fixed candidate class restricts}
\label{sec:finite-discussion}

A natural objection to fixing the candidate class $\cT_{\Lbar}$ in advance is that the true splits are not known ahead of time, so restricting the search to a pre-specified grid looks like trading nonparametric flexibility for an essentially parametric hypothesis space. The precise cost is narrower than that, and it is worth separating two different things a fixed, finite candidate class restricts: the analyst's \emph{search}, and the statistical \emph{model} in which $\theta_0$ is defined and efficiency is assessed. Only the former is restricted here: Theorem~\ref{thm:regular} and Proposition~\ref{prop:sparse-bound} (\S\ref{sec:instantiation1}) establish that doubletree is regular and efficient for the full nonparametric model, and does not exploit the smaller variance a genuinely parametric restriction on $e_0$ would buy.

A genuinely parametric restriction on $e_0$ and $\mu_0$ would be cashed in as
a lower variance; structural sparsity is not. That is the sense in which
fixing the candidate class is not the objection it first appears to be: it
buys interpretability, and the estimator declines the resulting information
rather than exploiting it.

What \emph{does} restrict the data-generating process is structural sparsity
itself (Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity}) -- that the true nuisances be
\emph{exactly} representable by some tree within the budget $\Lbar$. We do
not minimize this cost in general: Proposition~\ref{prop:P-instantiation1} requires the truth
to align exactly, not merely approximately, with some tree in the relevant
class.

The reason this restriction is not load-bearing for the paper's inferential
guarantees is Theorem~\ref{thm:anchor}: validity does not require structural
sparsity to hold, and it does not even require the anchor to be built from the
same interpretable machinery. The anchor need only be some valid, $\sqrt
n$-consistent estimator (\S\ref{sec:honest-manuscript}); it could be a standard AIPW estimator with
flexible black-box nuisances entirely unrelated to the displayed trees, and
the anchor interval's coverage guarantee is unaffected. The question ``what
if the fixed candidate class is wrong?'' therefore has an answer that does
not depend on the fixed candidate class at all: report the same two trees,
and let the anchor -- built however one likes -- absorb the risk. What is
lost when sparsity fails is efficiency and a point estimate guaranteed to be
unbiased, not validity, and the loss is explicit and quantified, not hidden,
in Corollary~\ref{cor:width}'s width.

\subsection{A special case: the single-outcome-tree plug-in}
\label{sec:single-tree}

Remark~\ref{rem:two-trees-why} separated two points about the propensity tree: that its use is an interpretability choice rather than a validity requirement, and that the propensity nuisance itself remains informative for the ATT. The following corollaries make both points precise in the concrete special case of a single outcome tree fit with no propensity tree at all.

\begin{corollary}[Single-outcome-tree plug-in as a special case of doubletree]
\label{cor:single-tree}
Fix the outcome tree's leaf-refit $\hat\mu=\hat\mu_{\tauhat_\mu}$ as in \S\ref{sec:trees}, fit with no propensity tree at all, and consider the treated-mean plug-in
\[
  \thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}
  \;=\;
  \frac{1}{n_1}\sum_{i:A_i=1}\bigl\{Y_i-\hat\mu(\bX_i)\bigr\},
  \qquad
  n_1=\sum_{i=1}^n A_i.
\]
For a leaf $\ell$ of $\tauhat_\mu$ write $|\ell|_1=|\ell|-|\ell|_0$ for its treated count, and let $\hat w_{\mathrm{tie}}(\bx)=|\ell_{\tauhat_\mu}(\bx)|_1/|\ell_{\tauhat_\mu}(\bx)|_0$ be the empirical treated-to-control odds within the leaf containing $\bx$. Then $\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}$ solves the doubletree estimating equation $\Prob_n[\psi(\bO;\theta,\hat e,\hat\mu)]=0$ for $\hat e=\hat w_{\mathrm{tie}}/(1+\hat w_{\mathrm{tie}})$, and in fact for \emph{any} leaf-wise-constant-on-$\tauhat_\mu$ choice of $\hat e$: it \emph{is} doubletree's own two-tree estimator with the propensity tree forced equal to the outcome tree, not an alternative estimator that avoids a propensity altogether. Because the point estimate is invariant to which leaf-wise-constant $\hat e$ is used, this identity alone does not yet pin down $\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}$'s asymptotic variance -- that requires the specific choice $\hat e=\hat w_{\mathrm{tie}}/(1+\hat w_{\mathrm{tie}})$ and the separate argument of Lemma~\ref{lem:single-tree-linear} below.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Within any leaf $\ell$ of $\tauhat_\mu$, $\hat\mu(\bx)=\hat\mu(\ell)$ is by construction the control-arm sample mean, so $\sum_{i\in\ell}(1-A_i)\{Y_i-\hat\mu(\ell)\}=0$ identically -- for \emph{any} constant multiplying it. Hence $\Prob_n[(1-A)\hat e/(1-\hat e)\{Y-\hat\mu(\bX)\}]=\sum_\ell \hat w(\ell)\cdot n^{-1}\sum_{i\in\ell}(1-A_i)\{Y_i-\hat\mu(\ell)\}=0$ for any leaf-wise-constant $\hat e$ on $\tauhat_\mu$, with $\hat w=\hat e/(1-\hat e)$; this includes $\hat e=\hat w_{\mathrm{tie}}/(1+\hat w_{\mathrm{tie}})$ as one case among many. The doubletree estimating equation therefore reduces exactly to $\Prob_n[A\{Y-\hat\mu(\bX)-\theta\}]=0$, whose solution is $\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}$ by definition.
\end{proof}

For $\tau\in\cT_{\Lbar}$, define $e_\tau(\bx)=\Prob(A=1\mid \bX\in\ell_\tau(\bx))$, $w_\tau=e_\tau/(1-e_\tau)$, and $\mu_\tau=\Pi^\nu_\tau\mu_0$ for the control-weighted leaf projection of $\mu_0$ defined in \S\ref{sec:trees}; write $\eta_\tau=(e_\tau,\mu_\tau)$ and let $\theta_\tau$ solve $\E[\psi(\bO;\theta,\eta_\tau)]=0$, with $\psi_\tau=\psi(\bO;\theta_\tau,\eta_\tau)$. Note $1-e_\tau(\bx)=1-\E[e_0(\bX)\mid\bX\in\ell]\ge c$ by Assumption~\ref{ass:causal}, for every $\tau$, so $\eta_\tau$ always satisfies Proposition~\ref{prop:bilinear}'s hypothesis. The tied estimator's population objects are these evaluated at the \emph{realized} partition, $\eta_{\mathrm{tie}}=\eta_{\tauhat_\mu}$, $\theta_{\mathrm{tie}}=\theta_{\tauhat_\mu}$, $\psi_{\mathrm{tie}}=\psi_{\tauhat_\mu}$ -- random-index objects in general, since unless $\cS_\mu$ is a singleton or the relevant quantities happen to agree across its members, they depend on which sufficient partition is selected.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform asymptotic linearity of the tied estimator]
\label{lem:single-tree-linear}
Suppose $\cS_\mu\ne\emptyset$, so $\Prob(\tauhat_\mu\in\cS_\mu)\to1$ by Proposition~\ref{lem:selection}. For fixed $\tau$, let $\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}(\tau)$ denote the tied estimator formed from the control-arm leaf refit on $\tau$. Then
\[
  \max_{\tau\in\cS_\mu}
  \left|
    \sqrt n\{\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}(\tau)-\theta_\tau\}
    -
    \sqrt n\,\Prob_n\psi_\tau
  \right|
  =o_p(1).
\]
Moreover, $\theta_\tau=\theta_0$ for \emph{every} $\tau\in\cS_\mu$: since $\mu_\tau=\mu_0$ for every $\tau\in\cS_\mu$ by Definition~\ref{def:sufficient}, Proposition~\ref{prop:bilinear}'s bilinear bias vanishes regardless of $e_\tau$, so the tied estimator's target is unambiguous even when $\cS_\mu$ is not a singleton. Substituting the realized $\tauhat_\mu$,
\[
  \sqrt n(\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}-\theta_0)=\sqrt n\,\Prob_n\psi_{\tauhat_\mu}+o_p(1)
\]
on $\{\tauhat_\mu\in\cS_\mu\}$. This is a \emph{random-index} expansion: $\psi_{\tauhat_\mu}$, and hence $\Var(\psi_{\tauhat_\mu})$, need not be the same number across different realizations of $\tauhat_\mu$ when $\cS_\mu$ has more than one element and $e_0$ is not constant across their leaves (Corollary~\ref{cor:single-tree-saturated} identifies the one case where it is). It does not, by itself, assert an unconditional central limit theorem with one fixed asymptotic variance; that additionally requires either $\cS_\mu$ to be a singleton, or $\tauhat_\mu$ to stabilize to a deterministic partition in probability.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $\cS_\mu\subseteq\cT_{\Lbar}$ is a fixed, finite set, it suffices to establish the expansion for each fixed $\tau\in\cS_\mu$ and take a maximum over finitely many such expansions. At fixed $\tau$, $\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}(\tau)=\sum_{\ell\in\tau}(|\ell|_1/n_1)\{\bar Y_1(\ell)-\hat\mu(\ell)\}$ is a smooth (ratio-of-sums) function of the finitely many leaf-wise treated counts, treated-arm means, and control-arm means, each an ordinary sample average converging at rate $n^{-1/2}$ to its population counterpart. A first-order Taylor expansion of this finite-dimensional smooth function gives asymptotic linearity at fixed $\tau$, with influence function equal to the efficient influence function of the ATT in the model that coarsens $\bX$ to its leaf $\ell_\tau(\bX)$ -- namely $\psi_\tau$, as defined above. Taking the maximum over the finitely many $\tau\in\cS_\mu$ preserves the $o_p(n^{-1/2})$ remainder, and substituting $\tau=\tauhat_\mu$ on $\{\tauhat_\mu\in\cS_\mu\}$ gives the final display.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Exact efficiency under saturated leaves]
\label{cor:single-tree-saturated}
Suppose $\Prob(\tauhat_\mu\in\cS_e\cap\cS_\mu)\to1$. For \emph{every} $\tau\in\cS_e\cap\cS_\mu$, $e_\tau=e_0$ and $\mu_\tau=\mu_0$ $\Prob$-a.s., so $\psi_\tau=\psi_0$ identically -- the influence function does not depend on which such $\tau$ is realized, so the random-index issue of Lemma~\ref{lem:single-tree-linear} does not arise here. Since $(\tauhat_\mu,\tauhat_\mu)\in\cS_e\times\cS_\mu$ in this case, Proposition~\ref{lem:uniform} applies directly to $\thetahat(\tauhat_\mu,\tauhat_\mu)=\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:single-tree}), so $\sqrt n(\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}-\theta_0)\rightsquigarrow N(0,V)$ for $V=\E[\psi_0^2]$, the same semiparametric efficiency bound attained by doubletree's own two-tree estimator (Proposition~\ref{prop:P-instantiation1}), with no propensity tree required.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[A super-efficient submodel estimator under coarsening and homoskedasticity]
\label{cor:single-tree-coarsening}
Suppose $\Prob(\tauhat_\mu\in\cS_\mu)\to1$ and $\cS_e\ne\emptyset$ as well -- jointly, Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity} -- so that the untied two-tree estimator attains $V=\E[\psi_0^2]$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:P-instantiation1}; the two hypotheses are compatible, and their compatibility is the point. By Lemma~\ref{lem:single-tree-linear}, $\theta_{\mathrm{tie}}=\theta_0$ \emph{exactly} regardless of which $\tau\in\cS_\mu$ is realized, and $\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}-\theta_0=O_p(n^{-1/2})$; absent partition stabilization, this is the random-index expansion of that lemma, not yet a statement of one fixed asymptotic variance.

Fix $\tau\in\cS_\mu$ and suppose, further, that $\sigma_0^2(\bx)$ is constant on every leaf of $\tau$ (within-leaf homoskedasticity of the control outcome). Then the fixed-partition tied estimator satisfies $\sqrt n\{\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}(\tau)-\theta_0\}\rightsquigarrow N(0,V_\tau)$ for $V_\tau=\Var(\psi_\tau)$, with
\[
  V_\tau\ \le\ V,
\]
strictly whenever $e_0$ is not $\Prob$-a.s.\ constant on some leaf of $\tau$ with $\sigma_{0,\ell}^2>0$. The treated-arm terms of $\psi_\tau$ and $\psi_0$ coincide exactly, since $\mu_\tau=\mu_0$ and $\theta_\tau=\theta_0$; the two variances therefore differ only through the control-arm term. Doubletree's own control-arm term is $\pi^{-2}\E[e_0^2\sigma_0^2/(1-e_0)]=\pi^{-2}\E[f(e_0(\bX))\sigma_0^2(\bX)]$ for the strictly convex function $f(e)=e^2/(1-e)$ ($f''(e)=2/(1-e)^3>0$); under homoskedasticity, the fixed-$\tau$ tied term is $\pi^{-2}\sum_{\ell\in\tau}\Prob(\ell)\,\sigma_{0,\ell}^2\,f(\bar e_\ell)$ for the leaf average $\bar e_\ell=\E[e_0(\bX)\mid \bX\in\ell]$, while doubletree's own term is $\pi^{-2}\sum_\ell\Prob(\ell)\,\sigma_{0,\ell}^2\,\E[f(e_0)\mid\ell]$; Jensen's inequality applied to $f$ within each leaf gives $\E[f(e_0)\mid\ell]\ge f(\bar e_\ell)$, with strict inequality whenever $e_0$ is not $\Prob$-a.s.\ constant on $\ell$. \textbf{Heteroskedasticity breaks this ordering}: if $\sigma_0^2(\bx)$ varies within a leaf and correlates with $e_0(\bx)$ there, the per-leaf comparison is no longer a within-leaf Jensen step, and the fixed-$\tau$ tied estimator can be less efficient than the untied estimator -- indeed substantially so (a two-point leaf with $e_0\in\{0.1,0.8\}$ equally likely and $\sigma_0^2\in\{1,0\}$ respectively already gives the untied term $\approx0.006$ against the tied term $\approx0.30$, roughly a fiftyfold reversal).

If, in addition, $\tauhat_\mu$ stabilizes to some deterministic $\tau_\star\in\cS_\mu$ in probability, the tied estimator's unconditional limit is $N(0,V_{\tau_\star})$ with $V_{\tau_\star}\le V$ under $\tau_\star$'s own homoskedasticity, matching Lemma~\ref{lem:single-tree-linear}'s stabilization condition; absent stabilization, $\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}}$ still converges at rate $n^{-1/2}$ to $\theta_0$, but no single fixed variance need describe its limit law across repeated realizations of $\tauhat_\mu$.

This ordering does not contradict Theorem~\ref{thm:regular}: for fixed $\tau_\star$, the fixed-$\tau_\star$ tied estimator is \emph{not} a regular estimator of $\theta_0$ in the full (unrestricted) model. Under within-leaf homoskedasticity, $\psi_{\tau_\star}$ is exactly the projection of $\psi_0$ onto the tangent space of the strictly smaller submodel in which $\mu_0$ is constant on the leaves of $\tau_\star$, so $V_{\tau_\star}$ is the semiparametric efficiency bound \emph{for that submodel}, not for the unrestricted model that Theorem~\ref{thm:regular} bounds; the tied estimator attains the submodel's (smaller) bound precisely by exploiting the restriction, which is exactly the local-alternative sensitivity of an estimator efficient only within a submodel: moving $\mu_0$ off the submodel introduces a first-order bias, the hallmark of an estimator that is efficient within a submodel but irregular outside it. The same mechanism explains why homoskedasticity matters: it is precisely the condition under which the unweighted within-leaf mean is the efficient estimator of the leaf's control mean, so that $\mu_{\tau_\star}$'s implicit weighting is optimal for the submodel; under heteroskedasticity it is not, and the projection argument no longer guarantees an improvement.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[The naive standard error under-covers]
\label{rem:single-tree-se}
Because $\hat\mu$ is itself estimated from the full sample, treating it as fixed when computing $\Var(\thetahat_{\mathrm{tie}})$ omits the nonnegative control-arm variance contribution $\pi^{-2}\E[\{1-e_0(\bX)\}\hat w_{\mathrm{tie}}(\bX)^2\sigma_0^2(\bX)]$ entirely -- the naive variance is the treated-arm term alone -- and so generically under-covers. Under Corollary~\ref{cor:single-tree-coarsening}'s hypotheses and partition stabilization, the correct asymptotic variance is $V_{\tau_\star}$; without stabilization, Lemma~\ref{lem:single-tree-linear} instead supplies a random-index expansion and does not identify one fixed number. Either way, one never actually escapes the propensity by omitting its tree: the correct standard error recovers a propensity-like term as a byproduct of the outcome tree's own leaf structure.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Distinct from a single shared-structure tree]
\label{rem:single-tree-distinct}
Corollary~\ref{cor:single-tree} and its consequences answer a different question from a single \emph{shared-structure} tree (one tree serving both nuisances via leave-one-out algorithmic stability, following \citet{ChenSyrgkanisAustern2022}): there both nuisances are refit on the \emph{same} partition and the target is a working-model estimand, not necessarily $\theta_0$ itself. Here only the outcome is fit by a tree at all -- the propensity is never estimated as an independent object -- and the target throughout remains the true ATT $\theta_0$. These are different mechanisms answering different questions and should not be conflated.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Relation to penalized linear methods}
\label{sec:lasso-comparison}

Lasso-based nuisance estimation provides a useful comparison. In the orthogonal and debiased-inference literature, nuisance functions are also estimated by penalized empirical risk minimization, typically by minimizing squared loss plus an $\ell_1$ penalty \citep{belloni2014,farrell2015,chernozhukov2018}. Importantly, validity there is not made conditional on exact support recovery or model-selection consistency: Neyman orthogonality lets the very same displayed (debiased) estimator remain valid regardless of whether variable selection succeeded, provided only that a product-rate condition on the nuisance errors holds, which is what avoids the nonuniformity associated with inference after selecting a model \citep{leeb2005}. Doubletree's own resolution is comparable in structure but not identical in mechanism: its displayed point estimate has no such built-in robustness -- its plain confidence interval is asymptotically valid only when structural sparsity holds (\S\ref{sec:honest-manuscript}) -- so parity with the debiased-inference route is restored by falling back to a second, non-interpretable anchor estimator when sparsity may fail, not by any orthogonality property of the displayed tree-based estimator itself. What doubletree shares with the Lasso route is only the two-tier shape of the resolution -- an efficient point estimate when the structural condition holds, a fallback that remains valid when it may not -- not the reason either estimator achieves it.

The assumptions that purchase fast nuisance rates differ. Lasso analyses typically impose a restricted-eigenvalue or compatibility condition on the design. For doubletree, the fast selection rate in Proposition~\ref{prop:selection-rate} comes from the positive population-risk margin created by exact structural sparsity: every candidate outside the sufficient class has strictly positive excess risk, and the finite candidate class then yields a fixed gap. Finiteness alone is not enough; without the margin, a union bound over the finite class gives an excess-risk rate that is too slow for efficient inference. Doubletree's route to a fast rate therefore requires exact, rather than approximate, structural sparsity and is not a free alternative to the compatibility conditions used for Lasso.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Return to the payment, triage, and sentencing settings that motivated doubletree. In each, an analyst deploying doubletree reports exactly two objects: the propensity tree and the outcome tree, each small enough to display in full, together with the point estimate and a confidence interval. The fidelity diagnostic of Proposition~\ref{prop:spectest} offers evidence on whether structural sparsity is plausible, informing how much confidence to place in the plain interval relative to the anchor interval's wider, approximation-aware width -- but it does not establish coverage for an interval chosen on the basis of its outcome, and its own operating characteristics away from the asymptotic regime are themselves unvalidated. Proposition~\ref{prop:spectest} establishes only that the diagnostic's size tends to zero and that it is consistent against alternatives with a unique pseudo-true partition (Assumption~\ref{ass:pseudo}) and nonzero limiting bias, both conclusions conditional on the anchor's own rate condition (Assumption~\ref{ass:rate}), which concerns the unobservable population nuisances and cannot itself be checked from data -- not what the diagnostic's actual size or power looks like at a sample size and leaf budget an analyst would use in practice. A reader who wants coverage unconditional on that choice can report the anchor interval throughout instead, which requires only that the anchor be $\sqrt n$-consistent and asymptotically normal (Theorem~\ref{thm:anchor}), at the cost of extra width whenever sparsity in fact holds. A reviewer of the decision -- a regulator, a judge, an auditor -- can always trace the point estimate to specific leaf-level contrasts in the two displayed trees, without reference to a black-box model, a held-out fold, or a stability argument across resamples, whether or not structural sparsity holds. Auditing the interval's width, rather than the point estimate, is a different matter: the diagnostic's own test statistic is built from the anchor, so running the diagnostic pulls the anchor into the audit trail regardless of which interval it ends up recommending, and a reviewer who wants to audit that anchor inherits whatever interpretability cost the analyst's choice of anchor carries -- the fold-multiplicity of interpreting several distinct fits if it is a cross-fit interpretable learner, or the opacity of a black-box model if it is not, since the anchor need only be $\sqrt n$-consistent and may be either. The audit trail this paper delivers is therefore complete for the point estimate in every case, and complete for the interval only when the analyst reports the plain interval on the strength of structural sparsity assumed outright, without running the diagnostic.

The tree representation itself is not limited to small trees: Remark~\ref{rem:saturated-general} (\S\ref{sec:trees}) shows the saturated class subsumes any model restricted to the same grid-measurable inputs -- including a generalized linear working model, against which the containment is strict, since a tree can represent a cross-coordinate departure from additivity on the working model's link scale that no choice of coefficients can. Doubletree restricts the search to a small leaf budget for legibility, and \S\ref{sec:finite-discussion} argues that restriction costs nothing in the statistical model. It can cost something in representation, however: a nuisance whose additive structure is spread widely enough across active covariates to need more than $\Lbar$ leaves is exactly a nuisance for which structural sparsity fails at that budget, and Theorem~\ref{thm:anchor} absorbs the resulting bias into the anchor interval's width (Corollary~\ref{cor:width}) rather than into the validity of the reported inference. Whether real nuisance functions spread additive structure this way is an empirical question this paper does not settle; the broader case that legibility need not cost accuracy in practice, on structured data with meaningful covariates, is one \citet{rudin2019} has argued forcefully. \S\ref{sec:lasso-comparison} compares the assumptions doubletree and penalized linear methods each need for valid and efficient inference.

The paper is stated throughout for the average treatment effect on the treated, not the average treatment effect, and this is a scope decision rather than an oversight. The bilinearity of the estimating equation's bias in the two nuisance errors -- the structure Proposition~\ref{prop:bilinear} establishes for the ATT score -- is not itself specific to the ATT: Appendix~\ref{app:ate} sketches how the same mechanism, there splitting into one term for each of the two outcome regressions the ATE's identification requires, plausibly extends the argument. That preliminary argument stops short of a selection-consistency result, a central limit theorem, or an anchor interval for the resulting three-nuisance case, so we make no claim of a proven extension -- only that the identification and the shape of the bias are worked out, and that the natural doubletree analogue there would be three trees rather than two, which would need its own interpretability accounting. One part of the paper is ATT-specific by construction, not by omission: the single-tree corollary family (\S\ref{sec:single-tree}) is stated for the ATT's efficient score and efficiency bound, drawing its interest from \citet{hahn1998}'s result that knowing the exact propensity score lowers the semiparametric efficiency bound for the ATT but not for the ATE; and Remark~\ref{rem:two-trees-why}'s second point, that the propensity nuisance itself remains informative for the ATT, rests on the same asymmetry (the remark's first point, that the propensity tree is an interpretability rather than a validity requirement, is not ATT-specific). Under the ATE that asymmetry disappears, so whether an analogous single-tree result holds there is a distinct question from the one Corollary~\ref{cor:single-tree} answers here; we do not assume an ATE analogue would subsume it. Carrying Appendix~\ref{app:ate}'s sketch through to a proven selection-consistency result, central limit theorem, and anchor interval, and settling what, if anything, survives of the single-tree family in the process, is future work.

What this paper has not done is show that doubletree performs well, or even adequately, on any real or simulated payment, triage, or sentencing dataset; every guarantee here is asymptotic and pointwise in the data-generating process, not a demonstration that the leaf budgets, cutpoint grids, and sample sizes typical of these applications fall inside the regime where the asymptotics are a good guide. Nor does the paper claim to match the representational flexibility of an unrestricted machine-learning ensemble: a random forest or gradient-boosted model faces no leaf budget and no analogue of Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity}, and can approximate a far wider class of nuisance functions without needing to represent any of them exactly. The comparison this paper defends throughout is the narrower one, against a generalized linear working model (\S\ref{sec:lasso-comparison}), and, at the saturated budget, against any model restricted to the same grid-measurable inputs (Remark~\ref{rem:saturated-general}). Extending the analysis to finite-sample behavior and to genuine misspecification (rather than the exact-versus-not-exact dichotomy of \S\ref{sec:instantiation1}) is the natural next step.

A real-data application is planned, not yet run. It targets the New York State Office of Mental Health Medicaid serious-mental-illness cohort, reusing in full the population, exposure, and outcome design of a companion paper's partial-identification application (adherence to antipsychotic medication in the twelve months following an index diagnosis as the binary treatment; total medical cost in the following twelve months as the outcome; covariates drawn from the cohort's own baseline table) so that the two papers analyze the same applied dataset under contrasting identification strategies -- point identification here, joint-sensitivity partial identification there. Whether the estimator's requirements -- covariates in binary form, and either the fixed-grid exact-sparsity condition of \S\ref{sec:instantiation1} or the cross-fitting fallback that does not require it -- are met by that cohort's actual covariate set is itself an open, checkable question rather than an assumption: it depends on real column-level data this manuscript does not yet have access to, and answering it is the first step of the application rather than a precondition assumed to hold. An applied comparison against the black-box and linear alternatives discussed in \S\ref{sec:lasso-comparison} is a further natural extension once that application exists.

\section{Extension to the average treatment effect (sketch)}
\label{app:ate}

The results above are developed for the ATT throughout; this appendix does not offer a complete
counterpart of them for a different estimand. It states the ATE analogue of \S\ref{sec:setup}'s
identification and efficient-influence-function machinery, and shows that the bilinear bias
structure underlying Proposition~\ref{prop:bilinear} carries over, split across two outcome
regressions instead of one. It stops there: no selection-consistency result, central limit
theorem, or anchor interval is derived for the resulting three-nuisance case, and none is claimed.

The ATE is $\theta_0^{\mathrm{ATE}}=\E[Y(1)-Y(0)]$. Identification requires only that both arms be
represented, $0<e_0(\bx)<1$ for (Lebesgue-)almost every $\bx$, so that $m_1$ is defined --
already strictly more than Assumption~\ref{ass:causal}, which permits $e_0(\bx)=0$. Efficient
estimation needs a uniform two-sided strengthening of this, $c'\le e_0(\bx)\le1-c'$ for every
$\bx\in\cX$ and some $c'\in(0,1/2)$ -- a possibly different, generally smaller constant than
Assumption~\ref{ass:causal}'s own $c$ -- since both treatment arms enter the efficient influence
function's denominators below: exactly the additional restriction Theorem~\ref{thm:regular} shows
the ATT does not need. Under consistency, ignorability, and at least the pointwise bound,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ate-id}
  \theta_0^{\mathrm{ATE}} = \E[m_1(\bX)-\mu_0(\bX)],
\end{equation}
using both outcome regressions -- $m_1(\bx)=\E[Y\mid A=1,\bX=\bx]$, already defined in
\S\ref{sec:setup}, together with $\mu_0$. Like the ATT's own identification result
\eqref{eq:att-id}, \eqref{eq:ate-id} involves no propensity; unlike the ATT's, it requires the
treated-arm regression $m_1$ as well as $\mu_0$. The propensity enters only through the efficient
influence function: for a candidate nuisance triple $\eta^{\mathrm{ATE}}=(e,m,\mu)$ -- one
outcome-regression function per treatment arm, plus the propensity --
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score-ate}
  \psi^{\mathrm{ATE}}(\bO;\theta,\eta^{\mathrm{ATE}})
  =
  m(\bX)-\mu(\bX)-\theta
  +
  \frac{A}{e(\bX)}\{Y-m(\bX)\}
  -
  \frac{1-A}{1-e(\bX)}\{Y-\mu(\bX)\},
\end{equation}
the standard efficient influence function for the ATE in the nonparametric model
\citep{hahn1998}. The natural doubletree analogue built on this score is three trees, not two,
one for each of $e_0,m_1,\mu_0$ -- though, echoing \S\ref{sec:discussion}'s own framing, whether a
propensity-free analogue of \S\ref{sec:single-tree} exists here is exactly the question the ATE's
lack of the ATT's propensity asymmetry leaves open, not one this appendix answers.

\begin{remark}[Bilinear bias, split across two outcome regressions]
\label{rem:bilinear-ate}
For any candidate $\eta^{\mathrm{ATE}}=(e,m,\mu)$ with $c'\le e(\bx)\le1-c'$ for every
$\bx\in\cX$, evaluating $\E[\psi^{\mathrm{ATE}}(\bO;\theta_0^{\mathrm{ATE}},\eta^{\mathrm{ATE}})]$
by the same conditioning-on-$\bX$ argument that produces Proposition~\ref{prop:bilinear} for the
ATT gives
\[
  \E\bigl[\psi^{\mathrm{ATE}}(\bO;\theta_0^{\mathrm{ATE}},\eta^{\mathrm{ATE}})\bigr]
  =
  \E\left[\frac{e_0(\bX)-e(\bX)}{e(\bX)}\{m_1(\bX)-m(\bX)\}\right]
  +
  \E\left[\frac{e_0(\bX)-e(\bX)}{1-e(\bX)}\{\mu_0(\bX)-\mu(\bX)\}\right].
\]
The bias is again bilinear in a propensity error and an outcome-regression error -- exactly the
mechanism Proposition~\ref{prop:bilinear} isolates for the ATT -- but now there is one such term
per treatment arm rather than one term in total (the control-arm term here is exactly
Proposition~\ref{prop:bilinear}'s own remainder, up to its $\pi^{-1}$ normalization). If every
nuisance were exactly tree-representable at some shared leaf budget (a three-nuisance analogue of
Assumption~\ref{ass:sparsity}, with a third sufficient class $\cS_m$ for $m_1$
alongside $\cS_e,\cS_\mu$, in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:sufficient}), both terms would
vanish exactly, echoing how sparsity zeroes out Proposition~\ref{prop:bilinear}'s single term for
the ATT. Whether the fixed-finite-class selection argument of \S\ref{sec:partition-recovery}
extends unchanged to three simultaneously selected partitions, and whether the resulting central
limit theorem and anchor interval carry through, is not verified here.
\end{remark}
